GT3 Performance Bar · console architecture audit · 7 September 2026

No record has an address

You said you were talking about the app overall, and you were right that I'd been fixing one screen at a time. So I surveyed the whole console — 170 components, 36,000 lines, 15 sections, 39 panels — looking for what the individual complaints have in common. They have one thing in common, and it is upstream of all of them.

There is no route or URL anywhere in this app that addresses a single record. ?s=<section> is the deepest link that exists. You can link to a screen. You have never been able to link to a person, an order, an event, or a batch.

What that costs

13core entities a person works with
1had a detail view openable from anywhere
8had no home at all
15places a name is printed and can't be tapped

Three of the remaining entities have a defensible home — customers, vendors, goals — and nothing links to any of them. CrmDetail is the best-designed record view in the codebase: order history off the all_orders spine, loyalty, perks, VIP proof. Its only defect is that the sole way to reach it is to already be looking at the customer list.

And one entity had no interface whatsoever. A customer could place a shop order that nobody on the crew could look at — shop_orders was touched by two API routes and zero components. The checkout even raised an alert with subjectId: orderId, pointing at a screen that did not exist. Closed below.

A link that half-worked, for months

Found inside TaskSheet — the app's best-architected component. Tapping the goal name on a task ran:

router.push("/crew?section=goals")

The section parameter is s, not section. And "goals" is not one of the fifteen sections — goals live under Command. Two wrong things in one string, landing you on My Day with no error and no clue. It's fixed, and deliberately not pointed at a #goals anchor, because no such anchor exists and a hash nothing handles is the same half-working link.

Related, and the reason the dead ends were even possible: lib/db.ts's Order interface omitted customer_id. The column has existed since customers were introduced. Every screen could print the name; none could open the person.

What shipped

Not a rewrite. The pattern already existed on exactly one entity — TaskSheet's provider plus openTask(id, source) — and this generalises it.

PieceWhat it does
lib/records.ts A record is a (kind, id). Parsing is strict — unknown kind, missing id, or a non-uuid all return nothing rather than opening an empty sheet.
RecordProvider One host above every screen. Opening writes ?r=kind:id, so a record survives a reload, the back button works, and you can send someone a link to a person instead of "go to Team, scroll down, tap the third row".
RecordLink One line per dead end. Degrades to plain text when there's no id, so a guest order never renders a button that goes nowhere.
CustomerRecord Loads a customer by id and hands it to CrmDetail. Rebuilds nothing — that view was already right, it was just unreachable.

It also preserves the rest of the URL, which isn't hypothetical: rebuilding a URL from scratch and dropping every other parameter is exactly what broke the hire deep link, and that took three attempts to find.

12 new assertions, all of them about the ways a stale or hand-edited link can be wrong — because the thing being replaced is a link that half-worked.

Item one, done: the order nobody could see

The storefront is open — 506 products imported, 6 published, 0 orders so far. The next one that lands is now visible.

The checkout route already said this out loud, in its own failure path:

"Apliiq submit failed … It's paid and queued — submit it by hand."

Queued where. The alert it raises carries subject_id = the order id and a link to /crew, which is the top of a six-thousand-line page. There was no queue. The alert named a place that did not exist — this audit's thesis, inside a single route.

Then, tracing every writer of the status column, four of its eight values turned out to be unreachable. Nothing anywhere wrote them:

StatusWhat wrote it, before today
paidNothing — checkout inserts needs_fulfillment directly.
needs_fulfillmentCheckout, on insert.
submittedCheckout, once Apliiq accepts.
in_productionNothing.
shippedThe Apliiq fulfilment webhook.
deliveredNothing.
refundedNothing.
canceledNothing.

They were the shape of a life the app could not actually give an order. All eight are reachable now, the legal moves between them are enforced in the database rather than in the screen, and cancelling or refunding one requires a reason that is stored with who did it and when. A bare status write recorded none of that, and shop_orders had no audit trail at all.

Recording a refund does not issue one. Card data never touches this app, by design — refunds happen in Square. That is the exact trap /api/orders/cancel fell into and had to be corrected for: it told customers "your refund is on the way" when all it had done was raise a staff alert. So the confirm says what it does and what it does not, and the Square link sits beside the button.

A refunded order now stays refunded. The failure this prevents is concrete: you refund in Square and mark it here, nobody cancels it at Apliiq, Apliiq prints and ships it anyway, and its webhook runs update shop_orders set status = 'shipped'. The refund is gone and the record says an order you gave the money back for is on its way. The guard deliberately does not throw — throwing would 500 the webhook, Apliiq would retry forever, and the tracking would be lost with it. The terminal status holds, the late report is written onto the order where a person will read it, and the tracking is kept.

51 new database assertions, executed against the real migration with the real check constraint — a state machine that emits a value the table refuses is a runtime error nobody sees until a customer's order is stuck. Two of them found defects in my own same-day work: a column testing for an orphaned product line, which on delete set null makes permanently impossible; and an age label that read "just now" for an order with no age, because Number(null) is 0. The queue is sorted on that number.

And what turned up on the way

Not part of this audit's finding. Found by asking, before building the screen, what a customer sees if they read one of these saved queries directly.

Measured against the live database, signed in as a real account with the default member role:

select count(*) from public.profiles;          --  1   security working exactly as designed
select count(*) from public.v_crew_person;     --  3   the entire crew
select count(*) from public.v_market_crew;     --  3
select count(*) from public.v_agreement_hours; --  1   hours logged on an operator agreement

One row from the table. Three from the view over the same table. That gap is the bug. A Postgres view runs as the database owner unless it is explicitly told otherwise, and the owner ignores row-level security — so every one of these was a side door around the protection the tables themselves were enforcing correctly.

40views in the database
35ran as the owner, readable by any logged-in account
11of those I wrote, in the four migrations before the fix
0after

Nothing leaked. All fifteen accounts today belong to the crew, so there was no customer to walk through the door. That is calendar, not design: the storefront is open, every signup mints an account with the member role, and the first customer to make one would have had it.

Twenty-one internal diagnostics are now closed to customers outright — seven of them read Postgres's own catalogues, which have no row-level security at all, so the usual fix would have protected them exactly nothing. The thirteen the app reads now honour the same rules as their tables. The crew census after the change is identical, view for view, all forty.

The migration asserts its own effect rather than claiming it — and that assertion earned its place on the first run. It named five views I had not checked, because my survey had asked "which unsafe views can a logged-out visitor read", and those five were the ones I had already decided were safe. The only ones I had stopped looking at were the only ones I had stopped looking at. The release gate now refuses any future view that repeats it, and it was proven to fail on a bad view before it was trusted to pass on a good one.
