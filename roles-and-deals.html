Roles & Operator Deals

Two questions, and they turn out to be one. What an operator can see is set by three database functions — not by the seven role names above them. What an operator is owed is set by a choice you haven't made yet, and one legal test you should run before you make it.

Part one

What each role can see and do

The app has seven role names. The database has three gates. Almost everything surprising below follows from that gap.

Permissions are not enforced by the role name. They're enforced by three SQL functions that every row-level security policy calls. A role name only matters insofar as one of these three returns true for it.

GATE 1 · the wide one

is_staff()

Everyone who isn't a member. Server, contractor, operator, event manager, admin, owner — all identical here.

role <> 'member'
or is_admin = true

GATE 2 · the middle

is_admin()

Admin and owner only. Note what's missing: event manager is not here, despite being "leadership" in the app.

role in ('admin','owner')
or is_admin = true

GATE 3 · the narrow

is_owner()

Owner only, and it ignores the legacy is_admin flag the other two honour.

role = 'owner'

The one that matters most for Atlanta

No permission check anywhere considers market or location. Zero of the 358 policies mention it. An Atlanta operator and a Greenville operator are the same principal to the database: anything one can read, the other can.

So the honest answer to "what will my Atlanta guy be able to see" is — today, with the operator role: every customer you have, every dollar of revenue, every margin, in both cities. The screens won't show him, but the API will. This is a governance decision you're currently making by accident.

Four findings you should fix regardless of the deal

01

Demoting someone doesn't revoke their access

There's a legacy is_admin boolean alongside the role. Two of the three gates still honour it, but the function that changes roles stopped clearing it 0031 vs 0023. Demote an admin and they keep full database admin — and the app still reads them as owner. Silent and permanent.

Do first. Cheapest, highest-consequence fix on this page.

02

Owner rights are granted automatically by email address

Two addresses are hardcoded into the sign-up trigger 0246:31. Anyone who signs up with one gets role='owner' at row creation, with no approval step. Sign-up is open to the public. Compromise of either mailbox is a full takeover.

03

No last-owner protection in the database

The only guard is client-side. Demoting the final owner is an unrecoverable lockout from every owner-only capability, short of direct SQL.

04

An admin can rewrite an accepted agreement

The operator-agreements policy is FOR ALL to any admin, with no status guard — so terms can be edited after acceptance. The event trail records it and deletion is blocked, so it's auditable rather than silent. Still: if the deal is a contract, the terms shouldn't be editable by anyone but you once signed.

What's genuinely well built, for balance

All 134 tables have row-level security on. Self-escalation through the profiles table is properly closed at the column level. Orders can't be forged as paid. Customer writes go only through controlled functions. The operator-agreement trail is append-only with no client write path at all. Tenant isolation is genuinely restrictive.

The gap isn't discipline — it's that is_staff() is doing the work the other two gates are named for.

Part two

Structuring the operator deal

Equity, investment, or time — what you're actually buying, what each structure costs you, and the one test to run before you sign anything.

Run this test first: your deal may legally be a franchise

The FTC Franchise Rule has a three-part test. If all three are true, you're selling a franchise — regardless of what the document is titled.

  • Brand association. He operates a business identified with your brand. You: yes
  • Significant control or assistance. You control the method of operation, or provide significant assistance — training, standards, operating manuals. You: yes — that's what the Academy is.
  • Required payment of $500+ in the first six months, to you or an affiliate. The FTC reads this broadly: it includes required purchases at a markup and retained rebates, not just fees. You: likely — a 30% royalty, or him buying supplies from you.

Three for three. Getting this wrong means FTC investigation, civil penalties, private lawsuits under state franchise laws, and potentially having to offer rescission — unwinding the deal and returning what he paid. Compliance means a Franchise Disclosure Document delivered 14 days before signing, and registration in some states.

This is the one thing on this page to take to an attorney before you sign anything. Not a general business lawyer — a franchise lawyer. It is also solvable: the structure can often be adjusted to sit outside the test, but only deliberately and only in advance.

The second flag: "contract employee" is two different things

The phrase itself is the risk. Someone is either an independent contractor or an employee, and you don't get to choose by writing it in the agreement — actual practice outweighs contract language. If you set his hours, direct his methods, require your training and supply his equipment, a regulator may read employee no matter what the document says.

The current federal test is the 2024 rule's six-factor economic-reality analysis, weighing the whole relationship with no single factor decisive. A 2026 proposed rule would return to weighting two "core" factors most heavily — control over the work, and opportunity for profit or loss — but as of now it is still pending and the 2024 rule governs. States are often stricter than the federal floor; Georgia and South Carolina need checking separately.

The irony worth noticing: the second core factor, opportunity for profit or loss, is exactly what your commission split creates. A real revenue share with genuine downside pushes toward contractor. A guaranteed draw with you directing the work pushes toward employee.

What are you actually buying?

Before the structure, the question underneath it. You're getting three different things from him and they have three different prices:

  1. Time and effort — he runs the market day to day. Paid for with commission, salary, or both.
  2. Capital — he funds supplies, equipment, or a vehicle. That's an investment, and investments expect a return and a return of principal.
  3. Risk transfer — he eats the loss in a bad month. This is the expensive one, and it's the one people forget to price.

Equity is the wrong instrument for the first. It's a plausible instrument for the second and third. Most operator deals that go wrong do so because one payment was used to buy all three, and nobody wrote down which.

The realistic menu

Revenue or commission share

What you've already built: 50% of commissions to him, 30% royalty back, 20% into the market — with a slider for who funds supplies.

He gets
Cash, monthly, tied to what he produces.
You keep
100% ownership. No dilution, no cap table, no partner in a sale.
Watch
The royalty is the leg most likely to trip the franchise test.
Reversible
Yes — ends when the agreement ends.

Profits interest

Real LLC equity, but only in future growth above today's value. If you liquidated tomorrow he'd get nothing.

He gets
Upside in what he helps build, taxed as capital gains if structured right. No tax at grant, with a time-sensitive 83(b) filing.
You keep
All value built to date. He shares only what comes after.
Watch
Makes him a K-1 partner, not a W-2 employee — self-employment tax, quarterly estimates. Needs a valuation and a real operating agreement.
Reversible
Only through vesting and forfeiture terms you write up front.

Phantom equity

A contractual right to a cash payout tied to value, with no actual ownership, no votes, and no seat.

He gets
A payout on a trigger you define — a sale, or a fixed date. Taxed as ordinary income.
You keep
A clean cap table and full control.
Watch
It's a future cash obligation. If Atlanta works, you owe real money at exactly the moment you're negotiating a sale.
Reversible
Only per its own terms.

Capital interest — actual ownership

He buys in, or is granted, a real percentage of the business as it stands today.

He gets
A share of everything, including what existed before he arrived.
You keep
Less. And you acquire a co-owner with rights.
Watch
A grant for services is taxable to him immediately at fair value. Issuing equity for investment can implicate securities law.
Reversible
Effectively no. This is the one you can't quietly undo.

The pattern most of these deals settle into

Cash for time, equity for risk — and never the same dollar for both. Concretely: a commission split that pays him for running the market now, plus a separate, vesting, market-scoped upside instrument that pays him if Atlanta is still worth something in three years. Two instruments, two documents, two triggers.

The reason to separate them is that they fail differently. If he leaves in month four, you want the commission to simply stop and the upside to simply not vest. One blended payment can't do that cleanly.

Questions to settle before a lawyer can help you

An attorney will be faster and cheaper if you arrive with these answered rather than discovering them at $400 an hour:

  1. Is Atlanta your business with him operating it, or his business under your brand? Everything else follows from this, including whether the franchise test even applies.
  2. Whose bank account does Atlanta revenue land in on day one? Right now the answer is yours — one Square merchant account, no split.
  3. Is he putting in money, or only time? If money: is it an investment expecting a return, or is he buying equity?
  4. What happens in month four if it isn't working — who has already spent what, and who eats it?
  5. If he leaves in year two having built something real, what does he walk away with?
  6. Do you intend to do this again in a third city? A structure built for one partner and a structure built to be repeated are different documents.
