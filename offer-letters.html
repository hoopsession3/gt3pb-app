Writing an offer that means what it says

You are hiring into two states at once. Greenville is South Carolina; Atlanta is Georgia. Almost everything about the letter is the same — and the handful of things that differ are the ones that decide whether the letter holds.

Question one

Which city — because that picks the state

The offer already carries a market, and until this week that field only decided which city’s name appeared on the letter. It decides more than that. Here is what actually differs between the two.

Greenville

South Carolina · founding market

Written notice at hire
Required. Every employer, no size threshold. The employee must be told in writing the normal hours and wages agreed upon, the time and place of payment, and the deductions that will be made.
Changing those terms later
In writing, at least 7 calendar days before it takes effect. Raises are exempt.
Keeping the letter from becoming a contract
A disclaimer in underlined capital letters on the first page. For a letter — as opposed to a handbook — no signature is needed. Whether it is conspicuous enough is decided by a judge, not by you.
Workers’ comp
Required at 4 or more employees, or once annual payroll passes $3,000. Part‑timers and family members count.

Atlanta

Georgia · opening on standing orders

Written notice at hire
No state equivalent. Nothing in Georgia law requires the notice South Carolina requires — which is an argument for writing every letter to the South Carolina standard rather than keeping two templates.
Changing those terms later
No statutory notice period. Set one yourself; the letter is where you say so.
The salary‑period trap
Georgia presumes the hiring runs for whatever period the wages are stated in. State pay as an annual salary with no at‑will language and you have handed a candidate an argument that they were hired for a year.
Workers’ comp
Required at 3 or more employees — full time, part time or seasonal. Officers and LLC members count toward the three even if they exempt themselves from coverage.

The practical read

Write one letter that satisfies South Carolina and it satisfies Georgia too, with one Georgia‑specific addition: an explicit at‑will sentence, because of the salary‑period presumption. That is the cheapest way to run two markets without running two templates.

The workers’ comp thresholds are the number to actually watch. Georgia’s is three, it counts part‑timers, and it counts you. An Atlanta lead plus two part‑time servers reaches it.

S.C. Code § 41-10-30 the normal hours and wages agreed upon, the time and place of payment, and the deductions which will be made from the wages — required of every employer, in writing, at the time of hiring.
S.C. Code § 41-1-110 A document issued by an employer shall not create an express or implied contract of employment if it is conspicuously disclaimed — underlined capitals, first page.
O.C.G.A. § 34-7-1 An indefinite hiring may be terminated at will by either party — but if wages are payable at a stipulated period, the presumption shall arise that the hiring is for such period.

Question three

Employee or contractor — and why the letter doesn’t decide it

You asked about a contract employee. That phrase is two different arrangements, and which one you have is decided by how the work actually happens, not by what the letter calls it. Calling someone a contractor in writing while setting their schedule, training them, supplying the gear and paying a guaranteed base produces an employee with a contractor’s paperwork — which is the expensive version.

Where the federal rule stands right now

Unsettled, and worth knowing before you build around it. The 2024 Department of Labor rule is still the regulation on the books for private lawsuits, but the Department has stopped enforcing it and told its own field staff to use the older 2008 framework. A replacement rule was proposed on 27 February 2026, comments closed in late April, and as of mid‑August 2026 it had not been finalised.

The proposal would return to weighting two factors above the rest: control over the work, and opportunity for profit or loss from the worker’s own initiative and investment. Those two are what the app’s classification check flags on, because they are the two that have mattered under every version of the test.

Georgia asks a harder question than the federal government does

For unemployment insurance, Georgia presumes services performed for wages are employment, and the burden is on you to show otherwise. The statute lists seven specific things that must be true about control: the person is not prohibited from working elsewhere, is free to accept or reject assignments without consequence, has no minimum hours or minimum order count, sets their own schedule, receives only minimal instruction and no direct supervision, has no territorial restriction, and is not required to behave in a manner the Commissioner reads as employment.

Read that list against a bar shift. Most hospitality work fails it on the first three.

O.C.G.A. § 34-8-35 · HB 389, eff. 1 Jul 2022 Employer bears the burden. Georgia broadened who counts as an employee and narrowed who can be a contractor.
29 C.F.R. Part 795 · status Sep 2026 2024 rule on the books, unenforced; 2026 replacement proposed 27 Feb 2026, not final.
FLSA salary floor $684 per week — about $35,568 a year — is still the federal minimum salary for an exempt white‑collar role. Neither Georgia nor South Carolina sets a higher one.
Confirm before you rely on it

The published summaries of Georgia’s test do not agree on one structural point: whether customarily engaged in an independently established trade is an alternative to the seven control factors or an additional requirement on top of them, and whether an IRS Form SS‑8 determination is a third path or a third condition. That difference decides whether contractor status in Atlanta is difficult or close to unavailable.

I could not resolve it from the sources I could reach, so I am not going to assert it. Put this exact question to a Georgia employment lawyer — it is a fifteen‑minute answer for someone who practises there, and it is the single fact that most changes how you staff Atlanta.

Also worth a lawyer’s eye

The at‑will disclaimer wording itself. South Carolina tells you the format — underlined capitals, first page — and leaves conspicuousness to a judge. That is a sentence worth having drafted once, properly, and then reused forever. I have deliberately not invented it for you.

The other conversation

Equity, investment, time — keep it out of this letter

You asked whether an Atlanta lead should be talked to in equity, in investment, or in hours. It is a real question and it belongs in a different document. An offer letter is an employment instrument; ownership and revenue‑share are a securities and partnership question, and mixing them means the cheap document drags the expensive one along with it.

The app already reflects that separation: operator agreements are their own table with their own approval path, their terms become final once accepted, and owner is deliberately excluded from the roles an offer letter can grant. The reasoning behind the split — and where a revenue‑share arrangement starts to look like a franchise to the FTC — is in Roles & Operator Deals.

The practical version: hire the Atlanta lead as an employee with a title, a salary and a commission tied to what they can actually move. If it works, have the ownership conversation as its own negotiation, with its own paper, six months in — when you both have evidence instead of projections.
